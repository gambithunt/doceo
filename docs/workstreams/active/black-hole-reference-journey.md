# Black Hole Reference Journey

Status: Draft reference experience  
Parent: [Doceo Concept Definition](./concept-definition.md)  
Topic: “How do black holes work?”  
Authored routes: **A** — Beginner · What would falling in feel like? (§1–§9)  
**B** — Beginner · Show me a real example · How astronomers found one (§10)

This journey is the experience fixture for the
[prototype](./prototype-validation.md). It is not a commitment to space as
Doceo's first subject domain — the [generation spike](./generation-spike.md)
decides that.

Two routes are authored deliberately. If both orientation-2 choices led to the
same scenes with different wording, the claim that orientation choices improve
relevance would be untestable, and adaptation is the core product bet. The two
routes teach **different focused ideas** through **different teaching patterns**,
which is what a real branch means.

## Purpose

Test whether Doceo's curiosity-first promise works as a complete experience:
personalized entry, two-tap orientation, immediate progressive media, a focused
lesson, an optional playful quiz, a clean ending, useful history, and quieter
adaptation on the learner's next visit.

This is an experience storyboard, not an implementation specification.

## Learning Intent

After the lesson, the learner should be able to distinguish two valid views of
falling toward a black hole:

- a distant observer sees the traveler slow, redden, and fade near the event
  horizon; and
- the traveler experiences crossing the event horizon in finite time without
  encountering a physical surface there.

The lesson uses a supermassive black hole as a thought experiment. Tidal forces
near a smaller stellar-mass black hole could destroy a traveler before the event
horizon; near a sufficiently large supermassive black hole, the strongest tidal
effects may occur later. Real accreting black holes also present lethal radiation
and material, so this is not presented as a survivable journey.

## 1. Entry

The home experience has no dashboard grid or onboarding panel. A dark,
atmospheric field contains one prominent invitation:

> What are you curious about?

The learner enters “How do black holes work?” Suggestions beneath the invitation
respond to their Doceo history. For a new learner, suggestions are editorial and
varied rather than personalized by invented assumptions.

On submission, the words remain on screen while nearby points of light begin to
bend subtly. The orientation emerges from the same scene; there is no route
change into a form.

## 2. Orientation

### Question one

> Where should we begin?

- **From the beginning** — I’m completely new
- **I know the basics** — Gravity, stars, space
- **Take me deeper** — Skip the usual introduction

Reference choice: **From the beginning**.

Each choice is a large tactile region with a distinct visual response. Tapping
advances immediately. A quiet back control allows correction; there is no
Continue button or progress indicator.

### Question two

> How should we explore it?

- **What would falling in feel like?**
- **Show me a real example** — How astronomers found one
- **What is actually inside?**
- **Surprise me**

Reference choice: **What would falling in feel like?**

The practical route is “Show me a real example.” Its wording is specific to this
topic rather than a generic “practical mode.”

## 3. Immediate Transition

The selected answer expands into the opening frame. The rest of the interface
falls away. Doceo does not show a spinner, percentage, generation checklist, or
AI animation.

Visible immediately:

> **Falling into a black hole**  
> Two people. Two very different views.

Narration and the first scene begin as soon as they are ready. Later scenes,
illustrations, and motion continue preparing out of view. If an upcoming scene is
not ready in time, the current illustration can breathe for longer or a simple
diagram can substitute; playback must not expose the generation pipeline.

## 4. Focused Lesson

Target duration: 90–120 seconds.  
Format: synchronized narration, captions, illustration, diagrammatic motion, and
sound design.  
Interaction: none during the lesson; the learner chose to watch, not be tested.

### Scene 1 — Choose the safer impossible trip (0–12s)

**Visual:** A tiny spacecraft passes a normal star, a stellar-mass black hole,
and then an enormous supermassive black hole. The final object fills the frame.

**Narration:**

> Imagine falling toward a supermassive black hole. We’ll choose a quiet one,
> without a blazing disk of hot material. This is still a thought experiment—but
> it lets us focus on what gravity does to space and time.

**Purpose:** Establish the scenario without implying a realistic safe journey.

### Scene 2 — A boundary, not a surface (12–29s)

**Visual:** The dark centre is surrounded by a thin, unlabeled boundary. As the
traveler approaches, the label “event horizon” resolves softly into view. No
hard shell or portal is drawn.

**Narration:**

> Around the black hole is an event horizon. It isn’t a wall or a surface. It is
> a boundary: after you cross it, every possible path forward remains inside.
> Even light can no longer reach the outside universe.

**Purpose:** Replace the common “cosmic hole with an edge” misconception.

### Scene 3 — Split the viewpoint (29–42s)

**Visual:** The composition divides without becoming a dashboard. One continuous
space scene shows a distant observer on the left and the falling traveler on the
right. Their clocks tick together at first.

**Narration:**

> Now watch the same fall from two viewpoints. One person stays far away. The
> other travels with the spacecraft. Gravity makes their clocks disagree.

**Purpose:** Introduce the single central comparison.

### Scene 4 — What the distant observer sees (42–66s)

**Visual:** Pulses of light leave the spacecraft. Each later pulse takes longer
to arrive, stretches toward red, and becomes dimmer. The distant observer's view
of the traveler appears to slow near the event horizon.

**Narration:**

> To the distant observer, signals from the traveler arrive more slowly. Their
> light is stretched, reddened, and weakened. The traveler seems to slow and fade
> near the horizon. The observer never receives a signal sent from inside it.

**Purpose:** Explain observable slowing without saying the traveler literally
freezes in their own experience.

### Scene 5 — What the traveler experiences (66–88s)

**Visual:** The split dissolves into the traveler's view. Their local clock ticks
normally. The event-horizon line passes behind them without a flash, impact, or
portal effect. The outside universe appears increasingly distorted.

**Narration:**

> For the traveler, their own clock feels normal. At a sufficiently large,
> quiet black hole, crossing the event horizon need not feel like hitting
> anything. They pass the boundary in a finite amount of their own time—but can
> no longer send news back out.

**Purpose:** Deliver the counterintuitive reveal accurately.

### Scene 6 — The danger is still real (88–104s)

**Visual:** A gentle head-to-foot gravity gradient becomes dramatically uneven
as the traveler moves deeper. The figure stretches into a simple line diagram,
not graphic body horror.

**Narration:**

> Deeper in, gravity can pull much harder on the nearer part of an object than
> the farther part. That tidal stretching is called spaghettification. For a
> smaller black hole, it could happen before the horizon.

**Purpose:** Introduce tidal forces while preserving the mass-dependent nuance.

### Scene 7 — End on the idea (104–116s)

**Visual:** Two clocks remain: one far away, one carried inward. The event horizon
becomes a quiet boundary between what can and cannot send information outward.

**Narration:**

> So which view is real? Both. Far away, you see the traveler slow and fade. For
> the traveler, the horizon passes and the journey continues. A black hole makes
> “what happens now?” depend on where you are watching from.

The final sentence completes, the image settles, and the lesson ends. No streak,
confetti, score, next-lesson carousel, or completion dashboard appears.

## 5. Optional Quiz

After a short visual pause, a quiet prompt appears outside the completed lesson:

> Want a 10-second check?

- **Yes, let me try**
- **Not now**

“Not now” closes the lesson immediately and records no mastery inference.

### Playful check

Prompt:

> Who experiences the traveler crossing the event horizon?

Two illustrated statements appear:

- **The distant observer:** sees the traveler slow, redden, and fade.
- **The traveler:** crosses the horizon in their own finite time.

The learner taps one, both, or neither under the instruction “Choose every view
that fits.” The correct response is both statements. Selection animates the two
clocks back into their respective viewpoints.

### Evidence handling

- **Both selected:** record evidence that the learner distinguished the two
  viewpoints.
- **Only distant observer selected:** record a possible “traveler freezes”
  misconception; future suggestions may include a short visual on proper time.
- **Only traveler selected:** record uncertainty about what signals reach a
  distant observer; future suggestions may include gravitational redshift.
- **Neither selected or quiz abandoned:** record participation only, not
  understanding.
- **Quiz declined:** record the preference and no learning evidence.

Do not show a numerical score. Feedback briefly reconstructs the correct visual
relationship, then ends.

## 6. Clean Ending

After the lesson—or after optional quiz feedback—the experience simply closes
back to the previous context. Do not interrupt with “What next?”, a forced save
choice, a rating request, or a recommendation panel.

History saving happens automatically and quietly.

## 7. History Record

The learner sees a reusable lesson entry:

> **Falling into a black hole**  
> Event horizons from two viewpoints · 2 min

The stored lesson should reopen with the same scene sequence, narration,
captions, and visual identity. Product architecture may preserve final assets or
a stable renderable lesson representation, but reopening must not produce a
different lesson unexpectedly.

## 8. Purposeful Memory Update

Example structured state after declining the quiz:

```text
interest: black holes
interest_cluster: space and gravity
stated_familiarity: beginner
chosen_angle: subjective experience of falling in
lesson_completed: event horizons from two viewpoints
quiz_preference_this_session: declined

```

Do not infer that the learner understood time dilation merely because they
finished watching.

## 9. Next Visit

The lesson is over, but the home suggestions are now better informed. They might
include:

- **Why does gravity change time?**
- **How do astronomers photograph something invisible?**
- **Could anything survive a supermassive black hole?**
- one deliberately unrelated curiosity to avoid trapping the learner in a topic

If the learner opted into the quiz, one suggestion may respond to their evidence.
Doceo does not display “recommended because you answered incorrectly.”

## 10. Route B — “Show me a real example”

The practical-example route. Authored as a genuine parallel branch: a different
focused idea, a different teaching pattern, different visuals, and its own
optional check.

### Why this route exists

The decision log records that the practical-example option needs evidence —
*test whether learners choose it and whether it improves comprehension*. It is
also the sharpest possible contrast with Route A: where A is a thought experiment
about subjective experience, B is about observation, measurement, and how anyone
knows any of this. A learner who replays with the other choice must get a
recognisably different lesson, and these two are unmistakable.

### Focused idea

> A black hole emits no light, so astronomers identify one by measuring its
> effects on things nearby.

**Learner outcome:** afterwards, the learner can say what kind of observation
counts as evidence for an invisible object — and why a dark patch of sky does
not.

**Teaching pattern:** evidence to inference. This pattern is not in the current
grammar library; authoring this route surfaced the gap. See the lesson grammar.

### Immediate transition

Visible immediately:

> **How we found a black hole**  
> You can't photograph nothing. So look at what it moves.

### Scenes

Target duration: 95–110 seconds. **Six scenes, not seven** — this idea resolves
in six, and the count follows the idea.

#### Scene 1 — You cannot photograph nothing (0–15s)

**Visual:** A telescope tips up toward the crowded galactic centre. The view
pushes in until a small, unremarkable patch of dark sky sits at frame centre,
quietly circled.

**Narration:**

> Black holes give off no light. There is nothing to point a camera at. So
> astronomers gave up looking for the object — and started watching what it does
> to everything around it.

**Purpose:** Frame the problem as a detection problem, and set up inference as
the method.

#### Scene 2 — Watch the stars, not the dark (15–38s)

**Visual:** The same star field, now moving. Over ticking years — 1995, 2002,
2012, 2020 — most stars barely drift, but a handful near the centre sweep out
long looping paths and come back around.

**Narration:**

> For nearly thirty years, astronomers tracked individual stars at the centre of
> our galaxy. Most drift almost imperceptibly. These few raced — swinging around
> a single point in the dark, and returning.

**Purpose:** Establish the observation itself before any interpretation.

#### Scene 3 — An orbit weighs what it circles (38–58s)

**Visual:** One star's path resolves into a clean ellipse. A marker sits at the
focus the star orbits — visibly empty. A mass figure resolves alongside it.

**Narration:**

> The shape and speed of an orbit reveal the mass at its centre. One star,
> called S2, completes a lap in about sixteen years. That orbit points to roughly
> four million times the mass of our Sun — inside a region smaller than our own
> solar system.

**Purpose:** Show the inference step explicitly. The measurement is of the star;
the conclusion is about the invisible thing.

#### Scene 4 — Ruling out the alternatives (58–76s)

**Visual:** Two candidate explanations appear beside the empty focus and are
struck through in turn — a tight swarm of ordinary stars, which scatters apart;
a single colossal star, which floods the frame with light.

**Narration:**

> Could it be a dense cluster of ordinary stars? A swarm that tight would have
> scattered long ago. A single enormous star? At that mass, we could not miss it.
> Nothing else we know of stays that heavy, that small, and that dark.

**Purpose:** Teach that evidence is what survives the alternatives — the step
learners most often skip.

#### Scene 5 — Then they photographed the shadow (76–98s)

**Visual:** Radio dishes scattered across a turning globe link into a single
Earth-sized array. The linked signals resolve into the familiar uneven orange
ring around a dark centre.

**Narration:**

> In 2019, radio telescopes across the whole planet worked together as one
> instrument, and produced an image of the galaxy M87's centre. Not a picture of
> the black hole — a picture of glowing gas bending around the darkness where it
> sits. In 2022, they did the same for our own galaxy's centre.

**Purpose:** Deliver the payoff observation while being precise about what the
image actually shows.

#### Scene 6 — End on the method (98–110s)

**Visual:** The three strands settle together in one frame: the looping orbit,
the mass figure, the ring image.

**Narration:**

> Nobody has ever seen a black hole. We know they are there because of orbits we
> can measure, a mass we can calculate, and a shadow we can photograph. That is
> what evidence for an invisible thing looks like.

The image settles and the lesson ends. No score, streak, or recommendation panel.

### Optional check — Route B

> Want a 10-second check?

Prompt:

> Astronomers watch three patches of sky. Which one suggests something massive
> and invisible?

Three small looping animations, chosen with one tap:

- **A** — stars drifting slowly along straight paths
- **B** — one star whipping around an empty point and returning
- **C** — a region of sky with no stars visible at all

The intended answer is **B**.

Feedback redraws B's orbit and marks the empty focus, restating that the motion
is the measurement. No score.

### Evidence handling — Route B

- **Chooses B:** record evidence that the learner distinguished inference-from-
  effect from absence-of-light.
- **Chooses C:** record a possible “darkness is the evidence” misconception —
  mistaking an absence of signal for a detection. Future suggestions may include
  a short visual on why empty sky is not evidence.
- **Chooses A:** record uncertainty about what orbital motion implies; future
  suggestions may include how orbits reveal mass.
- **Abandoned:** record participation only.
- **Declined:** record the preference and no learning evidence.

### History entry — Route B

> **How we found a black hole**  
> Finding the invisible by its effects · 2 min

## Visual and Interaction Rules

- The topic occupies the whole visual field; UI chrome remains secondary.
- Typography carries the narrative hierarchy and remains readable over motion.
- Captions are available by default and synchronized with narration.
- Motion communicates light paths, viewpoint, scale, and time; it is not merely
  decorative.
- Sound supports atmosphere but is never required for comprehension.
- Reduced-motion mode substitutes clear dissolves and static diagrams.
- Controls appear on intent and recede during playback; playback never hides an
  accessible pause control.
- The lesson must remain coherent if advanced media is delayed or unavailable.

## Questions to Test

- Does the first useful idea appear quickly enough to feel immediate?
- Does the progressively assembled lesson feel continuous rather than generated
  in pieces?
- Do learners understand why the two viewpoints differ?
- Does offering the quiz after the lesson preserve the feeling of curiosity?
- Does the clean ending feel satisfying or abruptly unfinished?
- Are next-visit suggestions enough to create continuity without a visible path?
- Would this complete experience be meaningfully difficult to reproduce with one
  good general-chat prompt?
