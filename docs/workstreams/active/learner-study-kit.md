# Doceo Learner Study Kit

Status: Ready to run  
Study: Experience prototype, round one  
Participants: Eight curious adults, roughly 25–45  
Session length: 10–15 minutes, plus an unprompted two-week return period  
Parent: [Doceo Experience Prototype](./prototype-validation.md)

## Purpose

This kit turns the prototype validation plan into a repeatable moderated session.
It is intentionally short. The facilitator should observe whether Doceo works
without first explaining what it is supposed to do.

The study asks whether a learner can:

1. begin from curiosity with almost no setup;
2. understand and use the two orientation choices;
3. learn one focused idea from the visual lesson;
4. choose freely whether to take the playful check;
5. finish, return home, and find the saved lesson; and
6. name a concrete difference from their usual use of chat, search, or video.

This round does **not** validate live generation, real adaptation, long-term
retention, production latency, or willingness to pay.

## Before Each Session

- Use the same deployed prototype build for all participants.
- Prefer the participant's normal device. Record phone or desktop.
- Open a fresh private browser window so History and suggestions begin empty.
- Confirm the prototype loads, but do not demonstrate it.
- Assign a participant ID such as `P01`; do not record unnecessary personal data.
- Ask permission before recording audio, video, or the screen.
- Have the observation sheet open somewhere the participant cannot see it.

Do not describe Doceo as adaptive, personalized, a course, a game, or an AI
teacher before the session. Those words would give away the experience being
tested.

## Facilitator Script

Text in quotation marks may be read aloud. Notes in brackets are instructions
for the facilitator and should not be read.

### 1. Welcome — about one minute

> Thanks for helping us test an early learning experience. We are testing the
> product, not you. There are no wrong actions. Please say aloud what you are
> noticing, expecting, and deciding as you go. I may stay quiet so I can see
> what is clear without help.

> Imagine you became curious about black holes and had a few minutes to explore.
> Starting on this screen, use the experience as you naturally would.

[Do not point to the input, suggestions, History, or any controls.]

### 2. Curiosity and orientation — two to three minutes

[Observe silently. Record the submitted wording, hesitation, selected starting
point, selected route, and any comments about what the choices might change.]

If the participant becomes stuck, wait ten seconds, then ask only:

> What would you expect to be able to do from this screen?

If direct help is still required, provide the smallest useful hint and mark the
step **assisted**. Do not turn an assisted completion into an unassisted pass.

### 3. Lesson — about two minutes

[Let the lesson play. Do not explain the visuals or controls. Record whether the
participant pauses, seeks, changes captions or audio, becomes distracted, or
comments on waiting, pacing, coherence, or relevance.]

When the narrative finishes, do not immediately speak. Allow the ending and
optional-check choice to stand on their own.

### 4. Optional check — up to one minute

[Allow the participant to accept or decline freely. Do not encourage the check.
Record the choice and any spontaneous reason. If accepted, do not help with the
answer.]

After the experience returns home, ask:

> In your own words, what was the main idea you just explored?

[Record the answer as nearly verbatim as possible. Grade it after the session
against the branch-specific evidence guide below.]

Then ask:

> What, if anything, shaped what the lesson showed you?

[This is a prompted answer. It can explain the participant's interpretation,
but it does not count as unprompted evidence that orientation felt adaptive.]

### 5. Home and History — about one minute

> Please find the lesson you just finished.

[Do not say “History.” Record whether the participant finds it without help and
whether the replay looks like the experience they expected to recover.]

Once the saved lesson is open, ask:

> Now return to the main starting screen.

[Record whether the Home control is found without help.]

### 6. Comparison — three to five minutes

Ask in this order:

1. > If you wanted to learn this normally, what would you have used?
2. > What felt different here, if anything?
3. > What part helped you understand the idea most?
4. > What felt confusing, slow, or unnecessary?
5. > How did the short activity at the end feel?  
   [Ask even if declined; do not use words such as quiz, test, score, or grade.]
6. > What would make you choose—or not choose—this again for another curiosity?

Do not ask whether Doceo is “better than ChatGPT,” whether it “felt personalized,”
or whether the participant “passed.” Those formulations prime the answers this
study needs to observe.

### 7. Close

> Thank you. You can keep access to this link for the next two weeks. We will not
> remind you to use it; open it only if you genuinely want to.

[Do not suggest a topic, ask for another lesson, or send a later reminder.]

## Branch-Specific Learning Evidence

Grade the participant's explanation for the idea their chosen route actually
taught. Accept plain language; do not require lesson vocabulary.

### Falling-in route

Count the central idea as understood when the participant communicates both:

- the traveler and a distant observer can experience or describe the fall
  differently; and
- the difference comes from how extreme gravity affects light and time near the
  event horizon.

A mention of “spaghettification” alone is not sufficient.

### Real-example route

Count the central idea as understood when the participant communicates both:

- astronomers can infer an invisible object from the motion or other effects it
  has on visible matter; and
- the evidence supports a massive compact object even before an image exists.

Saying only that “astronomers took a picture” is not sufficient.

Mark borderline explanations for later review rather than rescuing them during
the interview.

### Decision Thresholds

The experience is promising when:

- at least 6 of 8 complete orientation and the lesson without help;
- at least 6 of 8 explain their branch's central idea;
- at least 5 of 8 notice unprompted that their choices shaped the lesson;
- at least 5 of 8 name a concrete advantage over general chat, search, or video;
- at least 6 of 8 understand that the check is optional;
- no participant spontaneously describes the experience using scoring or mastery
  language; and
- confusion clusters around fixable interactions rather than the proposition.

Do not average away a serious failure. One repeated factual misunderstanding,
an inaccessible interaction, or a strong sense that the experience is merely a
decorated chat answer deserves investigation even if the counts pass.

## Route Coverage

Participants choose their route naturally during the primary task. Do not steer
them merely to balance the sample; doing so would contaminate the relevance
evidence.

If fewer than three participants naturally choose either completed route, run a
short secondary walkthrough after the scored session:

> Please try the same curiosity again, but choose a different way to explore it.

Use that walkthrough only to find usability and comprehension problems in the
underrepresented route. Do not count it toward unprompted adaptation or route
preference thresholds.

## Decision After the Study

- **Proceed:** thresholds pass, learning is visible, and participants name a
  distinctive advantage. Continue the generation spike before choosing
  production architecture.
- **Iterate:** value is present but failures cluster around fixable orientation,
  playback, ending, History, or accessibility details. Fix only the observed
  problems and repeat the affected tasks.
- **Reframe:** learners enjoy the presentation but cannot explain the idea or
  cannot distinguish it from a chat response. Revisit the learning interaction
  or product promise before adding subjects.
- **Stop:** the central proposition produces no meaningful learning or
  differentiation signal after a fair test.
